# simplon - working agreements

This central part is generated by the Simplon delivery kernel
(`./simplon.sh support claude-md`) and is the same in every product that has
the `claude` toolchain. Do not edit it here: change it in the kernel, and the
next kernel bump brings the change as a reviewable diff. The product's own
agreements follow in its own region below, and **where that region says
otherwise, the product's rule wins.**

## Two documents, one division of labour

simplon is delivered by the Simplon kernel. The product supplies the
data, the kernel supplies the mechanism.

- **The manifest is the product's, the mechanism is the kernel's.** A command's
  implementation belongs in the kernel as soon as it needs no product
  knowledge. Product data reaches the kernel through the manifest and the
  product context, never through an import. A product function that only
  forwards to the kernel is a shim, not a seam.
- **When a capability belongs to no single product, it belongs in the kernel.**
  A kernel change reaches simplon as a version bump of the pin, for every
  product at once.
- **The catalogue gives the task, the product names the command.** Command
  groups are the kernel's; a product contributes members to them. Adding a
  command to an existing group is a manifest edit. Cite a task by its
  coordinate, not by one product's command word.
- **The cost of moving a mechanism into the kernel is visible and accepted:**
  the help text a kernel task renders is the kernel's wording. The product keeps
  its own phrasing in the manifest's `help:`.

## The launcher is the one way in

`./simplon.sh` is the entry point. `./simplon.sh help` prints the assembled
command list. A fresh checkout needs nothing first: the launcher provisions
what it runs on.

- **Go through the launcher, do not hand-assemble its commands.** A command
  typed by hand carries none of what makes a gate reliable (caches, mirrors,
  pins, environment). The phantom failures it produces cost more than the
  keystrokes it saves.
- **A retired command name fails loudly; it is not kept as an alias.** An
  unknown-command error says "that word is gone" louder than a silent forward.
- **Know which invocations exit 0 for having done nothing.** A word that the
  kernel reads as an environment, or a teardown run against an instance that
  was never up, can print help or "done" and succeed. Never put such a form in
  a script or a runbook line.

## Quality goals, and the guard against rule creep

The kernel exists to give delivery **clear structure**, **extensibility** and
**reusability**, and to be **usable** while it does it. Everything else,
every rule, every refusal, every check, is a means. A means that costs more
flexibility than it buys is a bad one.

**Usable** is the easiest to lose, because nothing fails when it is missing. A
run should answer three questions without being asked: *which phase are we
in*, *what is happening in it*, and *what went wrong*. A run that reports how
many steps failed but not why is the tool declining to say what it already
knows.

**Guard against rule creep.** Rules are easy to add, each one looks justified
on its own, and nobody measures the sum. A rule exists only as long as the day
somebody reads it back; a rule that lives in one person's head drifts without
anyone breaking it, which is why writing it down is the fix.

## Three kinds of refusal - only one costs flexibility

Before adding a refusal, a validation or a guard, say which kind it is.

1. **Diagnosis.** The input is broken and the message says where and what the
   way out is. Costs nothing: without it the failure would be silent.
2. **Expression rule.** Something may no longer be said that could have been
   said, and what was said would have worked, merely differently. **Only this
   kind costs flexibility.**
3. **Decoration.** A message that says "something went wrong" is not a
   diagnosis; it is the defect wearing a hat.

Before building an expression rule:

- **Does it forbid something a consumer might legitimately want?** Measure it
  against every input you can reach, not the ones on this machine. A smaller
  population hides violations.
- **Would deleting be cheaper than guarding?** A second source you do not have
  cannot drift and needs no rule to watch it.
- **If an input violates it, what does the fix cost?** Not the one line, the
  surface: comments, docs, and every person who has to learn the new form. Ask
  before shipping, not after.

## The recurring defect

**An outcome that cannot tell "nothing to do" from "failed" is a bug.** It
recurs in every product. It is the one class of check that is always worth its
cost, because such a defect is green precisely because nobody looks. Look for
it in every `except`, every fallback, every empty list, every cached result.

The generalisation: **what meaning does a value carry on the far side of a seam
that it did not carry on this side?** `None` means "no verdict" to a caller and
"failure" to `rc != 0`; `True` means "yes" to a hook and `1`, failure, to an exit
code; `{}` from a swallowed exception means "nothing there" to everyone
downstream.

The resolution is always the same: exclude the ambiguous value at the seam, or
widen the range so each meaning gets its own value.

Corollaries that every product has paid for:

- **A missing capability is absent, never faked.** Without its configuration,
  an integration is wired as nothing and the caller hears why. A fake is
  chosen only by its own explicit switch, never inherited from a missing or
  misspelt setting. A typo must not buy a working system full of invented data.
- **A fix must not invent the missing capability.** When fixing a swallowed
  failure, also assert that the absent thing stays absent; otherwise the fix
  moves the defect and stays green.
- **A failed probe is not a result.** Do not cache it as one.
- **Presence is not capability.** A keyword in a binary, a flag in a help text
  or an accepted command does not prove the thing works. Probe the smallest
  real instance.
- **A run that hangs is neither green nor red.** A missing time limit is a
  missing verdict.

## Working practices

- **Measure, do not assume.** If you state something about behaviour, you ran
  it. Where an assumption is not testable, say so rather than hiding it. Keep
  measurement series apart: a run against the real system and a run against a
  staged failure must never be mixed.
- **A stub confirms exactly the assumptions written into it.** It cannot find
  the recurring defect. Prefer a real instance; record its limits explicitly
  where the next reader will find them. A limit known only to whoever hit it
  is not recorded.
- **See it red.** A new assertion that has never failed is not yet an
  assertion. Break the property it claims to protect and watch it fail. A
  green run proves nothing about a branch nobody reaches.
- **A green verdict must come from a command that ran the thing.** A teardown
  that tore down the wrong instance, a gate that skipped, a build that never
  reached the code: each reports success and verifies nothing.
- **Report findings, do not patch them away.** A defect found against a real
  system is worth more than the quick fix. Record it with its measurement,
  then decide.
- **Do not type a number or a population into prose.** A count typed into a
  document is wrong on the first day nobody checks it; compute it, or point at
  where it is computed. A number about a set is two questions: is the count
  right, and is it the set being talked about? Prose about a population ages
  the same way.
- **A quoted error text is a second source for a string**, not prose. Pin it
  against the real message.
- **Prove "unused" with the compiler or the test suite, not with grep.**
- **Search the solution's vocabulary before declaring a feature missing.**
- **Record rejected alternatives with the trigger that reopens them.** A
  decision is not relitigated without reason; the reason should be countable.

## Changing code

Bias toward caution over speed; for trivial tasks, use judgement.

- **Think before coding.** State assumptions. If several interpretations exist,
  present them; do not pick silently. On a real tradeoff, give the options with
  evidence and let the maintainer decide. Do not block on a question: do
  everything that does not depend on the answer first, then ask where the
  answer is needed.
- **Simplicity first.** The minimum code that solves the problem. No features,
  abstractions, configurability or error handling for scenarios that cannot
  occur. (Missing input from outside is not such a scenario: see diagnosis.)
- **Surgical changes.** Touch only what you must. Match the surrounding style.
  Unrelated dead code: mention it, do not delete it. Remove only what your own
  change orphaned. Every changed line traces to the request.
- **Goal-driven execution.** Turn a task into a verifiable goal ("fix the bug"
  becomes "write a test that reproduces it, then make it pass"). For
  multi-step work, state the plan with one verification per step.

## Skills and subagents

- **Check for a matching skill before acting**, including before a clarifying
  question. Process skills (brainstorming, systematic debugging, test-driven
  development, writing and executing plans, verification before completion,
  code review) go first; implementation skills carry them out. A lookup is not
  a task. Load skills on demand, one per task, never all upfront.
- **No fix before the root cause is found.** Reading the code first often
  produces facts a long lab run cannot.
- **Use subagents for fan-out reads** and for review before a change goes up.
  Relay what matters from their report; the user never sees it.
- **A subagent that writes needs its own worktree.** Two writers in one
  checkout corrupt each other.
- **Never give one brief contradictory merge instructions** (an auto-merge
  label and "do not merge" together).

## Git, commits and pull requests

- **Commit messages, issue text and PR bodies go through a quoted heredoc or
  `--body-file`, never inline.** Backticks inside double quotes run as shell
  substitution.
- Commit or push only when asked. Work on a branch, never on the default
  branch.
- **Read the issue list and the open pull requests before opening a ticket or
  building one.** Check who wrote a ticket before implementing it.
- A pull request that should close a ticket says `Closes #<n>` in its body; a
  house abbreviation of the ticket closes nothing.
- Generated contracts and generated files: regenerate, fix what breaks, and
  commit the regenerated artefact with the change that caused it.

## Working with the maintainer

- Lead with the answer. No preamble, no motivational padding, no closing
  summary. Blunt over hedged.
- Do not explain basics they already know. Bullets for lists, prose for
  analysis.
- Never write the em dash (U+2014) in anything you write: use a hyphen, a
  colon, parentheses or two sentences.
- Verify current facts (versions, releases) before stating them. If unsure, say
  so; never fabricate.
- Conversation and explanation in German; the product's technical artefacts in
  English. Where the line falls for commits, documentation and user-facing
  text is the product's decision and is stated in its own region below.
- Pending decisions go to the maintainer as a list of choices with the
  evidence for each.

## The four quality goals

Simplon exists to give a delivery pipeline **clear structure**,
**extensibility** and **reusability**, and to be **usable** while it does it.
Those four are the point. Everything else — every rule, every refusal, every
check — is a means, and a means that costs more flexibility than it buys is a
bad one.

**Usable** is the newest of the four and the easiest to lose, because nothing
fails when it is missing. Three questions a run should answer without being
asked: *which phase are we in*, *what is happening in it*, and *what went
wrong*. Measured against those, a run that reports how many steps failed but
not why (#49), or a tree with no durations (#52), is not a small omission —
it is the tool declining to say what it already knows.

**Guard against rule creep.** Rules are easy to add, each one looks justified
on its own, and nobody ever measures the sum. Every refusal had a measured
cause; the total was never weighed. It is weighed now — see #48.

**Do not restate the count here.** si#48 found that the `grep`-level number
this paragraph used to carry (61) was the wrong population: six of those
raises run at plan or run time, or only re-raise. The live count, split by
kind, is computed by `tests/test_refusal_census.py` — walking out from the two
places a manifest enters the kernel — and published on `with-what/rules.md`. A new
refusal with no census entry turns that suite red until somebody says which
kind it is, which is the point — a number typed into a document instead is
wrong on the first day nobody checks it, and this repository has now proved
that five times, twice in this very file.

**The census only counts what it is pointed at, and it is no longer pointed by
hand.** si#61 found sixteen load-time refusals sitting outside it, because the
population was a list of modules and `tasks/testrun.py` was not on it — a
census whose whole purpose is that the sum cannot grow quietly, growing
quietly. The repair then was to add the module to the list. si#53 stopped
repairing the list and derived it — a manifest enters this kernel at two seams,
`manifest.load(text)` and `ProductContext.manifest_data()`, and the population
is everything raised on the way from either to a decision with no tool
consulted in between — and the derivation found **six more modules**, nearly
doubling the total without one new rule being written. **A count that is
computed rather than typed is still only a count of what you pointed it at.**

Two things follow, and both are assurances rather than advice. A module that
reads the manifest is either walked or named in `READS_INLINE` with the reason,
so a new manifest reader cannot join quietly. And the sentence "everything else
in the kernel refuses at run time — a task that cannot reach its tool, an image
without a pin", which stood at the head of the census and on
`with-what/rules.md`, was **false**: the pin is decided on a manifest string
with nothing running. Prose about a population ages exactly the way a number
does, and nothing was checking it.

## Three kinds of refusal — only one costs flexibility

Do not lump these together. Before adding a refusal, say which kind it is.

1. **Diagnosis.** A manifest is broken and the message says where. Costs
   nothing — without it the failure would be silent. *Example:* a coordinate
   the catalogue does not carry.
2. **Expression rule.** A product may no longer say something it could have
   said, and the manifest it wrote would have produced a working product —
   merely a different one. **Only this kind costs flexibility.** *Examples:*
   #33 (one manifest form instead of two), #34 (a phase name in a coordinate
   is the placement), #43 (`env_groups` may not contradict the platform).

**Self-binding is not a third kind — it is an expression rule's reach.** The
first draft of this file listed it separately and put #34 under both, which is
how the mistake showed. Ask instead how far a rule reaches: does it hold the
kernel's own catalogue too (#34 checks over the *merged* tree, so it does), or
does it exempt the kernel from what it asks of a product? Measured in #48 and
again in #61, and **the split is on `with-what/rules.md`, not here** — every
count this paragraph used to carry has gone stale, the last two within a day of
being typed. Most expression rules bind the kernel with the product; a few sit
exactly on the platform/product seam; and **none exempts the kernel** any more.
There was one, `check_every_task_is_used`, and si#53 struck it — measuring the
exemption is what ended it. It was the only expression rule with no measured
cause in ticket, commit or docstring, and over every reachable manifest — this
kernel's own and the five in `surface.CONSUMERS` — it had never refused
anything.

**The third reason once given is withdrawn**, and how it fell is worth more
than the reason was. si#48 said the rule would refuse *14 of the kernel's own
22 catalogue tasks*. si#83 read the signature —
`check_every_task_is_used(flat, product_tasks)` — and the catalogue's
coordinates were never handed to it. **The count was right over a set nobody
had looked at**, the fourth time in this repository, which is why a number
about a set is two questions and not one: is the count right, and is it the set
being talked about?

What stands in its place is a measurement rather than a label:
`test_every_merged_reach_expression_rule_ruled_on_the_kernels_own_declarations`
loads this manifest against the real catalogue and asks each rule what it was
handed. Every rule that claims to hold the kernel really ruled on the
catalogue's own placements. An exemption is not automatically wrong. An
unexamined one is — and examining this one is what showed there was nothing
left to defend. `test_no_expression_rule_exempts_the_kernel_from_itself` holds
the zero.

## Before building an expression rule, answer these

- **Does it forbid something a product might legitimately want?** If so,
  which product, and what does it do instead? #34 showed *zero violations
  across both manifests on this machine* — and #48 later measured all **six**
  real manifests through the GitHub API and found one violation the smaller
  population had hidden. The bar is every manifest you can reach, and you can
  reach them all without a checkout; #37 and #47 had already shown how.
- **Is it diagnosis or expression rule?** Diagnosis needs no such
  justification.
- **Would deleting be cheaper than guarding?** A second source you do not
  have cannot drift, and it needs no rule to watch it. First applied in #46.
- **If a manifest does violate it, what does the fix cost that product?** Not
  the manifest line — the surface. #34 moved netctl's `monitor accept` to
  `test accept`, and its maintainers measured the real bill: the manifest is
  one line, the old command name is in eight comments and docstrings and an
  architecture document, and every user who types it has to learn the new one.
  "One violation" and "one line" are not the same number, and only the product
  can tell you which it is. Ask before shipping, not after.
